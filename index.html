Lesson 1: Macroeconomic indicators

Systemic risk is the chance that distress in one part of the financial system spreads and damages the real economy. Macro indicators are the slow-moving early-warning signals that tell you when the system is becoming fragile.

The core indicators

IndicatorWhat it measuresWarning sign
Credit-to-GDP gapPrivate credit relative to its long-run trend (used by the BIS and the Basel countercyclical buffer)Gap above about 10 percentage points
Yield curve slope10-year minus 2-year government yieldInversion (negative slope) often precedes recessions

No single indicator is reliable. Credit and asset-price booms tend to build before a crisis, while market-based measures such as volatility react once stress has started. Good monitoring combines both.

Lesson 2: Networks and principal components

Institutions are linked by loans, derivatives and shared holdings. Two lenses help: networks show who is exposed to whom, and principal component analysis (PCA) shows how much of the system moves together.

Network contagion

In a network, each bank is a node and each exposure is a directed link. When a borrower fails, its lenders lose the exposed amount. If that loss exceeds a lender’s capital, the lender fails too, and the shock propagates. Highly connected nodes and thin capital buffers make cascades more likely.

Principal components and the absorption ratio

PCA decomposes a covariance matrix into factors. The absorption ratio is the share of total variance explained by the first few principal components. When it rises, assets are moving together, diversification is weakening, and shocks spread faster.

Absorption ratio for 10 assets

All assets share the same pairwise correlation ρ. The first eigenvalue is 1 + 9ρ, and each of the other nine is 1 − ρ.

Lesson 3: Conditional and illiquidity risk

Ordinary VaR looks at a firm in isolation. Conditional measures ask a different question: how bad is the system when this firm is in trouble, and how bad is this firm when the system is in trouble?

The key measures

  • CoVaR: the value-at-risk of the financial system, conditional on one institution being at its own VaR. ΔCoVaR is that figure minus the system’s VaR when the institution is at its median state. It captures the institution’s contribution to systemic risk.
  • MES (marginal expected shortfall): the institution’s expected loss on days when the system is in its worst tail. It captures exposure to systemic risk.
  • SRISK: the capital an institution would need to raise to function normally after a severe market decline. It combines size, leverage and long-run MES.
  • Illiquidity risk: forced sales in thin markets push prices down, which hits other holders of the same assets and creates a spiral. The Amihud measure (average |return| ÷ dollar volume) is a common proxy for market illiquidity.

Lesson 4: Systemic risk meets machine learning

Machine learning can pick up nonlinear patterns and process huge, messy data sets. But crises are rare, so the main challenge is trading off missed crises against false alarms.

Where ML helps

  • Early-warning classifiers: logistic regression, random forests and gradient boosting predict whether a crisis will start within a horizon, often finding nonlinear threshold effects.
  • Dimension reduction: sparse PCA, autoencoders and clustering compress hundreds of series into a few risk factors.
  • Text and news: natural language processing turns central bank reports and news flow into sentiment indices.
  • Network inference: LASSO-based methods estimate connections from return data when exposures are not observable.

Where it struggles

  • Few events: there are only a handful of crises, so flexible models overfit easily.
  • Regime change: the next crisis rarely looks like the last, which weakens out-of-sample performance.
  • Interpretability: regulators need to know why a model raises an alarm. Tools like SHAP values and partial dependence plots help.
  • Evaluation: accuracy is misleading with rare events. Use recall, precision and AUC, with time-based validation.
